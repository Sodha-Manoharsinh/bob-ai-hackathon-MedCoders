# Problem Statement

## Background

This problem exists in the healthcare domain, where hospital doctors need to
review large volumes of patient records before ward rounds, referrals, and
discharge. Complex patients with multiple admissions may have 50–200 pages of
records spread across different departments, including clinical notes, lab
reports, medication information, and investigation results.

## The Problem

A hospital doctor can spend around 2 hours per day reading and summarising
patient records. Important clinical events, medication changes, and outstanding
investigations can be buried within large amounts of unstructured information,
making it difficult to quickly identify the information needed for patient
care.

Referral letters are also often written from memory, which can result in
important information being missed. This can contribute to avoidable
readmissions and repeated investigations.

## Who is Affected

The primary users affected are hospital doctors and other clinicians who need
to review patient records and prepare information for ward rounds, referrals,
and discharge summaries.

Patients are also indirectly affected when important information is missed or
when previous investigations have to be repeated because relevant information
is difficult to locate within their medical records.

## Why It Matters

The problem creates a significant time burden for clinicians, with hospital
doctors spending around 2 hours per day reading and summarising patient
records.

For complex patients, reviewing 50–200 pages of records can make it difficult
to identify key events, medication changes, and outstanding investigations
quickly.

The challenge statement also highlights the wider financial impact of
avoidable readmissions and repeated investigations, which cost the NHS more
than £1 billion annually.

Reducing the time required to understand patient records while making
important clinical information easier to identify could help improve the
efficiency of existing medical workflows.

## Why Existing Solutions Fall Short

The current workflow relies heavily on clinicians manually reviewing large
amounts of unstructured medical documentation and then creating summaries or
referral letters from the information they have reviewed.

This approach is time-consuming and makes it possible for important details to
be overlooked. Writing referral information from memory can also create gaps
in the information passed between healthcare professionals.

The core challenge is therefore not simply storing medical records, but
quickly turning large amounts of fragmented and unstructured information into
a clear, useful representation that clinicians can review and use.
